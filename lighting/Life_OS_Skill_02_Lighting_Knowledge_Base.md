## 1. Purpose

Learn to **see, predict, and control light**.

The long-term goal is simple:

> Enter any location, understand the available light quickly, and know where to place the subject and camera—or how to change the light—to create the intended image and feeling.

This is not a collection of lighting recipes. We learn **why light behaves the way it does**, test it in real situations, and turn repeated understanding into intuition.

## 2. Core Learning Rule — Connect Topics

**Do not teach lighting as isolated definitions or force every topic through a prediction/experiment loop.** Whenever a new idea appears, explain what previous ideas it builds on, which other topics it changes, and why those relationships matter for a real shot.

Our connected map starts with **light source → light reaches a surface/subject → light enters the lens → camera exposure and image**. Build outward into direction, bounce, reflection, shadow, contrast, apparent size, diffusion, subject position, blocking, camera position, mood, and story. Refer back to earlier lessons when a new one clarifies them.

The link must be real, not an invented association. Use whichever teaching format makes the idea easiest to understand: a plain explanation, analogy, practical scene, diagram, video, comparison, or — only when needed — an experiment. No fixed sequence is compulsory.

### Lesson 01 — Why camera, light, and reflector are connected

- **Light and image:** A camera forms an image using light that reaches its lens and sensor. For visible objects, much of that light has reflected or scattered off surfaces; luminous objects such as lamps can send light directly toward the lens.
- **Covering the lens:** A hand covering the lens blocks the scene light, so the scene appears very dark or black. This is not the camera refusing to capture; the light carrying scene information cannot reach the sensor.
- **Why a reflector works:** A white or silver surface redirects some incident light onto a subject. The subject then reflects/scatters some of that added light into the camera. This can brighten the darker side of the face and change shadow depth and contrast.
- **Why the connection matters:** Reflector position, surface type, light direction, face orientation (blocking), lens view, exposure and intended mood all participate in the **same image-making process**. A black surface can reduce bounced fill (negative fill) and deepen shadows.
- **Real-life example:** Beside a window, one side of a face may be brighter. Placing a white reflector opposite the window can add light to the shadow side. The exact effect depends on geometry, surface reflectance and available light.

**Carry this connection forward:** Whenever we learn bounce, fill, negative fill, diffusion, hard/soft light, contrast, exposure or blocking, explicitly point back to this physical explanation — and explain what the new concept adds.

## 3. Main Rule

> **Do not memorize lighting setups. Learn why the setup works.**

A setup is useful only when we understand what each light, surface, subject position, and camera choice is doing.

## 4. How We Read Light

When studying a location or light source, consider:

**Source → Direction → Size → Distance → Intensity → Quality → Colour → Surface → Subject → Camera → Exposure → Image**

These are not independent. Changing one can change the final image.

## 5. Scene Reading

Before adding equipment, first read what already exists.

Ask:

### Light
- Where is the light coming from?
- How strong is it?
- Is it hard or soft?
- What colour is it?
- What surfaces are reflecting it?

### Space
- Where is the depth?
- Which backgrounds are useful?
- Which areas are bright or dark?

### Subject
- Where can the character stand or move?
- What happens to the face when the character moves?

### Camera
- Where can the camera go?
- What does the camera see from that position?
- Can moving the camera or subject solve the problem before adding another light?

### Story
- What should the audience feel?
- Does the lighting support that feeling?

## 6. Controlled Indoor Storyboard Rule

AI-generated images must **not invent production capability**.

When a real room/reference image is provided:

1. Use the real room geometry and visible objects as constraints.
2. Use only equipment that is actually available unless a proposed addition is clearly labelled.
3. Do not silently add, remove, or relocate furniture, practical lights, decorations, or architecture.
4. If something should be removed or moved, state it as a production suggestion first.
5. Do not assume a light can produce a brightness, spread, softness, colour, or falloff that has not been demonstrated.
6. If output is unknown, treat the intended look as a **hypothesis**, not a guaranteed result.
7. Test the real setup before locking the final storyboard.

**Rule:**

> **Prove the light first. Visualize the final look second.**

A storyboard describes what the audience should see. A lighting plan describes how we will physically try to create it. They are related, but they are not the same thing.

## 8. Optional Experiment Method

Use a controlled experiment **when it helps resolve a real question**, not as a required step in every lighting lesson. For controlled experiments:

1. Choose one question.
2. Keep everything possible unchanged.
3. Change one main variable.
4. Predict what will happen before recording.
5. Shoot a short test.
6. Compare prediction with the real result.
7. Explain why it happened.
8. Record the practical rule learned.
9. Later test the rule in a real scene.

The purpose is not to collect experiments. When used, an experiment can help verify a connection or clarify an uncertain result; it is only one of several learning tools.

## 9. Optional Practical Exercise — Distance

**Question:** What changes when the same light moves closer to or farther from the face?

Keep the subject, camera, room, light settings, and basic direction unchanged.

Test several practical distances.

If doing this exercise, observe face brightness, shadow appearance, background relationship, and overall feeling. Compare the recordings. You may predict beforehand if that will help, but prediction is **not compulsory**. Link the results back to distance, apparent source size, exposure, and contrast.

## 12. Documentation Rule

Keep this artifact **simple, precise, and unambiguous**.

When recording a lesson or experiment:

- Prefer plain language over impressive vocabulary.
- Define technical terms when they are first needed.
- Record what was actually observed, not what was expected.
- Separate facts from assumptions.
- Separate tested rules from untested ideas.
- If later evidence changes an earlier understanding, correct the earlier section instead of preserving a confusing rule.
- Keep enough context that the lesson can be understood months or years later without relying on the original conversation.
- For each new concept, explicitly identify meaningful links to existing lighting lessons and relevant filmmaking/camera/story concepts, and revisit older sections when necessary; do not turn this into a mandatory checklist or experiment.
