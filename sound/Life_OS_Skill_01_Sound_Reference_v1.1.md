How to Use This Manual
This is not a compressed cheat sheet. It is a reconstruction manual. The goal is that even if months or 
years later a concept feels forgotten, reading the explanation, examples, corrections, and questions 
should rebuild the original understanding instead of forcing you to restart Sound from zero.
The manual begins at Lesson 02 because Lesson 01 already exists as a separate detailed PDF. 
However, Lesson 01 memory anchors are repeated wherever they are needed so later concepts 
remain understandable by themselves.
Use this manual in four modes:
 Study mode: read one lesson, close the PDF, and explain it from memory in your own words.
 Pre-task mode: before editing or shooting, recall the relevant lessons without looking, then use 
the PDF only to repair gaps.
 Review mode: after finishing a reel, identify which lessons you actually used and where your 
decision-making failed.
 Life OS / AI mode: give this manual with raw footage or a reel brief and explicitly ask the assistant
to analyze and design sound according to this system, not a generic cinematic-edit template.
The learning method is designed around retrieval, not passive rereading.
Learning Philosophy Recorded From the Journey
The learning method that emerged during these discussions is deliberately slower than a normal 
tutorial. Understanding something once is not treated as mastery. A lesson is repeatedly recalled, 
applied to new imaginary scenes, recognized in professional examples, and later used in real footage.
The important realization is that forgetting is often not complete erasure. A concept may still exist in 
memory but be slow to retrieve. Therefore the training target is not only knowledge; it is retrieval

Lesson 2 - Sound Perspective
The audience can feel distance through sound even when the source itself does not change.
DISTANCE -> PERSPECTIVE | LOUDNESS != DISTANCE | ROLE != PERSPECTIVE
The starting confusion was: if a sound becomes louder or grabs attention, does that make it close? 
The answer is no. Perspective is the perceived spatial relationship between the audience and the 
sound source. Foreground/background is the sound's storytelling role. These are separate controls.
A dog bark can be the same identity in three versions: three streets away, across the road, or 
extremely close to the mouth. The identity remains “dog bark.” What changes is the audience's 
relationship to it: far, medium, close.
What perspective changes
 Far sounds tend to feel less intimate and contain fewer tiny details from the source.
 Medium sounds feel clearly present in the scene but not physically beside the listener.
 Close sounds can expose tiny texture: breath, cloth, gravel, mouth detail, finger movement, object 
contact.
These are practical tendencies, not rigid laws. Editing can exaggerate or deliberately violate natural 
perspective for storytelling.
Critical correction: loudness is not distance
A quiet phone vibration directly beside your head can feel extremely close. Thunder can be 
massively loud yet still feel kilometres away. Therefore “louder = closer” is not a reliable rule.
Loudness is one cue among many. Perspective can also be suggested by detail, clarity, reflections, 
environmental blending, filtering, reverberation, timing, and the visual context.
Critical correction: attention is not distance
A distant waterfall can become the most important sound in the entire scene while still feeling far 
away. If the character stops and the editor makes the waterfall clearer, its role may change from 
ambience to foreground. Its perspective can remain far.<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 5 / 37>LIFE OS | SKILL 01: SOUND | REFERENCE MANUAL

Lesson 3 - World, Action, Focus
Instead of adding random sounds, build a scene by assigning jobs.
WORLD -> WHERE? | ACTION -> WHAT HAPPENED? | FOCUS -> WHAT MATTERS 
NOW?
Lesson 03 moved from analyzing one sound to constructing a whole scene. A realistic location 
contains many sounds, but a useful film mix organizes them by function.
World
World sounds make the location exist. In a kitchen this could be fan or refrigerator hum, distant 
traffic, birds through a window. In a mountain guesthouse it could be a heater, filtered wind, distant 
voices, birds, or an exterior waterfall depending on whether the window is open.
World is not defined by continuity. A sound can appear occasionally and still establish place. The 
reason it belongs to WORLD is that it contributes to the sense of where the audience is.
Action
Action sounds come from physical events: zipper, footsteps, cup placement, key jingle, drawer 
movement, shoe contact, camera strap sliding, bedsheet movement. A key refinement from 
discussion was: “picking up a camera” is not itself a sound. Ask what physical contacts occur when 
the camera is picked up: body rubbing bedsheet, strap sliding, buckle clicking, hand gripping.
This “what physically touches what?” question is a gateway into Foley thinking.<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 7 / 37>LIFE OS | SKILL 01: SOUND | REFERENCE MANUAL

Focus
Focus is what matters now. If a phone vibrates during ordinary packing and the story suddenly turns
toward the phone, the vibration becomes focus. Focus commonly overlaps with foreground, but 
“focus” is useful as a scene-building label: what deserves the audience's attention at this moment?
Hierarchy
The important correction was that a scene can contain many sounds, but they should not all demand 
equal attention. A rich mix does not make the audience an observer; an unorganized mix does. Many 
layers can coexist if some remain soft, some provide texture, and one or two guide attention.
Analogy: a song may contain drums, bass, voice, guitar, synth, and background vocals at the same 
time, yet the singer remains easy to follow because the mix has hierarchy.
Reconstruction test
Hotel-room example: AC hum and faint traffic = WORLD. Backpack zip and shoes = ACTION. Knock on
the other side of the door = FOCUS. If you can rebuild that scene, the lesson is remembered.<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 8 / 37>LIFE OS | SKILL 01: SOUND | REFERENCE MANUAL

Lesson 4 - Rhythm and Expectation
Sound has timing. Repetition teaches the brain what should happen next.
RHYTHM -> WHEN? | REPETITION -> EXPECTATION | BREAK -> CHANGE
Lesson 04 shifted the question from “what sound?” to “when does it happen?” The exact same sound 
can create different feelings depending on timing, spacing, repetition, and interruption.
Train example
A night train produces a repeated “ka-dunk... ka-dunk...” pattern. Before anything happens, this can 
do two jobs simultaneously: establish the train world and create a predictable rhythm. As the brain 
learns the pattern, the repetition can become comfortable and stable.
If one expected ka-dunk disappears, tension can begin before any dramatic event. The audience feels 
the missing beat because the brain predicted it. A later BANG becomes more powerful because the 
rhythm prepared the audience first.
Peaceful use of the same rhythm
The same train rhythm can support peace rather than suspense. If the pattern continues steadily 
under shots of a window, tea, passing landscape, and a resting face, repetition communicates 
movement and stability. The sound itself can become the beat of the reel without music.
Mountain-climb example
Repeated footsteps and heavy breathing communicate continuing effort: the journey is not over. At 
the summit, one final step followed by no footsteps and only wind can communicate arrival. No 
dialogue is needed. The break itself tells us the phase changed.
A key refinement: wind is not silence. The footstep/breathing rhythm can stop while the world 
continues. This is a rhythm break and simplification, not necessarily total silence.<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 9 / 37>LIFE OS | SKILL 01: SOUND | REFERENCE MANUAL

Lesson 5 - Contrast
A sound can become powerful because of what surrounds it, not only because of what it is.
CONTRAST -> DIFFERENCE | A SOUND GETS POWER FROM WHAT SURROUNDS IT
Contrast means a meaningful difference between before and after, or between one element and its 
surroundings. Quiet to loud is only one form. Busy to simple, many to one, fast to slow, far to close, 
normal to strange, or one layer disappearing can all create contrast.
Festival to side street
In the crowded festival there may be music, drums, shouting, vendors, vehicles, firecrackers, and 
footsteps. Turning into a nearly empty side street leaves footsteps, breathing, and a distant festival 
drum. The drum may become more noticeable even though it remains far because far fewer sounds 
compete with it.
This is an important correction: the drum does not become powerful because it became close. Its 
surroundings changed.
Peaceful vs dangerous with the same location
Quiet does not automatically mean peaceful. A quiet street can be terrifying. For peace, keep stable 
footsteps, gentle breathing, distant drum, light wind, and no abrupt breaks. For danger, isolate 
footsteps, break the rhythm, reduce the distant festival sound, and bring breathing forward. Same 
visuals, different sound relationships.
Birds stop, water and wind continue
When birds suddenly stop but water and wind continue, the whole scene is not “silence.” One layer 
has disappeared. The audience had become accustomed to birds + water + wind. Removing birds 
creates a selective absence: expectation breaks, the before/after difference becomes contrast, and 
attention rises.<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 11 / 37>LIFE OS | SKILL 01: SOUND | REFERENCE MANUAL

Lesson 6 - Integration - Build the Full Sound World
The previous lessons stop being isolated definitions and become one continuous audience 
experience.
SCENE = WORLD + ACTION + FOCUS + SPACE + RHYTHM + CHANGE
Lesson 06 is not a new category so much as an integration rule. A scene should feel continuous even 
though many lessons are operating at once.
Mountain guesthouse exercise
The scene began in a mountain guesthouse: wake up, open window, far waterfall, pack bag, walk 
downstairs, open front door, cold wind, begin walking, motorcycle approaches from behind.
WORLD inside the room could include heater/fan and filtered exterior sound. ACTION could include 
bedsheet movement, window latch, zipper, stairs, hand brushing plants, grass or branch contact. 
When the window opens, the waterfall can become FAR + FOREGROUND. The indoor world drops 
and the outdoor world becomes clearer, creating contrast.
The motorcycle can move in perspective: far -> medium -> close -> medium -> far. It can remain 
foreground the whole time if the story continues to care about it.
A crucial correction: change emotion without changing the plot
When asked to make the scene dangerous, the first instinct was to add someone screaming. That 
changes the story. A stronger sound-design exercise is to keep the visuals and events the same and 
change only sound decisions: harsher wind, birds stopping, isolated footsteps, clearer breathing, 
irregular engine rhythm, motorcycle slowing near the subject.
Rule: when asked to change emotion, first try changing the sound design before inventing a new 
dramatic event.
Reality gives everything; film chooses
This lesson later became even clearer during the rooftop test. Real locations can contain many 
simultaneous sounds. Film sound is not a copy of everything at equal importance. It is a deliberate 
organization of what the audience experiences.<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 13 / 37>LIFE OS | SKILL 01: SOUND | REFERENCE MANUAL

Lesson 7 - Sound Motivation
Before adding a noticeable sound, ask why it deserves to exist.
MOTIVATION -> WHY? | MOTIVATED VS ARBITRARY
A beginner can add whooshes, hits, heartbeats, risers, and booms simply because they sound 
cinematic. Lesson 07 replaces that habit with a reason-based decision.
Real-world motivation
A physical event causes the sound: door closes -> thud; shoe hits floor -> step; bike passes -> engine; 
zipper moves -> zip. The sound belongs naturally to the action.
Story motivation
The sound may already exist in the world, but the story gives us a reason to emphasize it. At a 
railway station, many announcements may remain ambience. If one announces that the character's 
train is delayed, that specific announcement becomes important.
Important correction from the test: “the audience notices it” is the result, not the motivation. The 
deeper reason is that the event changes the scene; therefore the sound is emphasized; therefore 
audience attention follows.
Emotional motivation
A sound can express the internal experience rather than literal physical reality: after shocking news, 
office sounds become muffled, breathing becomes huge, heartbeat appears. The world did not 
physically change; the sound represents the character's state.<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 14 / 37>LIFE OS | SKILL 01: SOUND | REFERENCE MANUAL

Unrealistic does not mean wrong
A giant BOOM on a mouse click would be arbitrary in a normal realistic office action. But if the 
mouse click submits a resignation, the exaggerated boom can become story-motivated. The useful 
distinction is not realistic vs unrealistic; it is motivated vs arbitrary.
Reconstruction test

reconsider it. If the answer is physical cause, story importance, emotional experience, comedy,

Lesson 8 - Sound Transitions
Sound can carry the audience across visual cuts, places, and time.
TRANSITION -> CONNECT | WHAT SOUND CAN CARRY ME INTO THE NEXT 
MOMENT?
1. Continue across the cut
Old B.Tech friends laugh. The image cuts to present-day office, but laughter remains for a fraction of 
a second before fading. The picture says NOW while the sound briefly says THEN. This can create 
nostalgia and emotional continuity.
2. Next sound arrives before its picture
We still see the office, but a gym weight CLANG starts before the image cuts to the gym. The sound 
pulls us into the next scene early.
Railway-station realization: the train can be approaching but not yet visible. A distant horn or engine 
arrives in sound before the train appears in image. This is an early-arriving transition. Perspective is 
a separate question: that early sound may be far, medium, or close.
3. One sound transforms or matches another
A friend hits the shoulder in an old clip: THUMP. Cut on that impact to the present-day hand gripping 
or setting a dumbbell: THUD / CLACK. The timing and sound shape connect two unrelated actions. A 
transition can therefore be both a visual match and a sound match.
Natural sound instead of generic whoosh
Door thud, zipper, footstep, breath, laugh, train horn, plate, or gym impact can connect shots more 
personally than a generic whoosh. The question is not “where do I put a transition effect?” but “what 
sound already belongs to these worlds and can connect them?”<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 16 / 37>LIFE OS | SKILL 01: SOUND | REFERENCE MANUAL

Lesson 9 - Present, Memory, Internal Sound
Sound can tell the audience not only where a source is, but when or where in experience it 
belongs.
PRESENT -> NOW | MEMORY -> THEN | INTERNAL -> INSIDE
Present sound
The sound belongs to what is physically happening in the current scene: office ID beep, keyboard, 
spoon, AC, footsteps.
Memory sound
A sound belongs to another time but is heard over the present image. Example: eating alone in the 
office while faint old friends' laughter enters. The laughter is not happening physically in the office. It
belongs to THEN.
When the edit cuts into the actual old B.Tech clip, the same laughter becomes ordinary present sound
inside that past scene. When the edit returns to current time, allowing laughter to linger for a 
fraction of a second can make the memory feel as though it followed the character back into the 
present.
Internal / subjective sound
Heartbeat, exaggerated breathing, muffled surroundings, or a voice becoming distant can express 
what the moment feels like inside the character. This is not necessarily memory. It belongs to 
internal experience.
Foreground can come from memory
A memory sound can be foreground even though its physical source does not exist in the current 
scene. Foreground is about intended audience attention, not physical presence.<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 18 / 37>LIFE OS | SKILL 01: SOUND | REFERENCE MANUAL

Applied Foundation A - What the A Quiet Place 
Professional Example Added
After Lessons 1-9, a professional example was watched specifically to add visualization. The strongest
takeaway was not a forced attempt to identify every previous lesson. It was a new sound-design 
principle: the physical source used to create a sound does not have to match the object shown on 
screen.
Examples observed included ordinary low-cost materials and devices recorded to help create 
fictional creature movement and other effects. A cabbage-like twisting texture could contribute to an 
imagined muscle contraction; an electrical device could contribute to a creature effect. The audience 
never needs to know the raw source if the processed result convincingly serves the screen event.
Core principle:
REAL SOURCE != SCREEN OBJECT. Use any source material that contains useful sonic qualities, then 
record, select, layer, and process until the final sound belongs emotionally and physically to the 
scene.
This is not permission to use random sounds. Lesson 07 still applies. The source may be surprising, 
but the final result must be motivated.
Why this matters for independent travel creation
The professional example showed that creative raw material can come from inexpensive objects, not 
only premium libraries. This supports the larger Life OS goal of self-reliance: instead of always asking
“where can I download the exact sound?”, learn to ask “what can I record around me that contains 
the texture I need?”<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 20 / 37>LIFE OS | SKILL 01: SOUND | REFERENCE MANUAL

Applied Foundation B - Sound Properties / Qualities
A major doubt appeared after the Foley exercise: if the scene already says “disgusting crunch under a
shoe,” why ask what properties or qualities are needed? The answer is that “crunch” is a broad label. 
The useful design question is: what kind of crunch?
Qualities are audible characteristics
Examples of qualities: sharp, brittle, wet, dry, heavy, tiny, metallic, hollow, dense, soft, scratchy, 
smooth, close, distant, short, long, sudden, sustained. These are not technical plugin settings yet. They
are descriptions of what the final sound should feel like.

Why qualities come before objects
If you jump directly from scene to object, experimentation can become random. If you first define the
final qualities, experiments become targeted. You know what you are listening for. “Apple” is not the 
goal. “Dense wet crunch” is the goal; apple is one candidate source.
A practical design sequence
1. What should the audience feel or understand?
2. What should the final sound feel like? Describe qualities in ordinary words.
3. Which real objects might contain one or more of those qualities?
4. Record several variations: different force, speed, distance, surface, angle.
5. Listen critically. Reject most of the material if necessary.
6. Layer or process useful pieces until the result serves the scene.
This converts experimentation from random guessing into guided experimentation.<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 22 / 37>LIFE OS | SKILL 01: SOUND | REFERENCE MANUAL

Applied Foundation C - Sound Is Not Only Post-Production
Another important doubt was whether video must come first because Foley and sound design are 
done in post. The correct answer is more complete: precise Foley often needs picture, but sound 
thinking spans the entire production.
Pre-production
Before shooting, sound can influence planning: this scene should become sparse when the letter 
opens; we need clean station ambience; the creature should feel organic, not mechanical; footsteps 
are story-important, so we need a plan to capture or recreate them.
Production
During shooting, capture what reality gives you: dialogue, room tone, ambience, wind, water, traffic, 
location-specific detail, and useful action sounds. For travel creation, production sound is essential 
because unique locations cannot always be recreated later.
Post-production
After picture exists, construct and refine: Foley, additional effects, libraries, layering, cleaning, EQ, 
perspective manipulation, transitions, subjective sound, music, and mixing.
Can sound come first?
Yes. A train rhythm, voice recording, song, environmental texture, or sonic concept can inspire the 
visual edit or even the scene itself. “Picture -> sound” is common, but “sound -> picture” is also valid. 
The requirement is an intentional relationship between the two.
Permanent rule:
Sound is thought about before, captured during, and constructed/refined after.<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 23 / 37>LIFE OS | SKILL 01: SOUND | REFERENCE MANUAL

Practical Record - Rooftop Listening Verification
The first real-world listening test was completed on a building rooftop. This mattered because the 
earlier lessons had mostly been imaginary scene exercises. The rooftop test showed what the ears 
actually noticed without a script controlling the environment.
Observed world
The listener was seated on the rooftop looking toward a tall building with other surrounding 
buildings. Audible elements included birds, children playing on another building, and a motorcycle 
from below.
Perspective recognition
Children were perceived around medium distance and naturally grabbed the most attention, 
producing a reasonable analysis of MEDIUM + FOREGROUND. A bird moved through different 
distances: initially far/background, later medium/foreground, closer/foreground, then away again. 
This demonstrated that real-world perspective can be heard dynamically.
Rhythm and contrast in reality
The children produced an ongoing, imperfect pattern. When that activity broke and a motorcycle 
became audible from below, the change stood out. This was an unplanned real-world example of 
ongoing pattern -> break -> new sound -> contrast.
The difficult filmmaker-choice question
The test became difficult when asked which one sound should deliberately become foreground for a 
15-second rooftop reel. The initial feeling was that every direction contained sound and that using 
everything would prevent the audience from being immersed.
The correction was one of the most important realizations in the whole learning journey: many 
sounds do not automatically destroy immersion. Equal competition destroys guidance. The 
filmmaker can keep a rich world while organizing hierarchy.
Permanent principle:
REALITY GIVES ME EVERYTHING. I CHOOSE WHAT THE AUDIENCE EXPERIENCES.
Possible stories from the exact same rooftop
Peaceful city afternoon: keep distant city and birds, let children become the clear foreground. 
Feeling: life is happening around me.
Lonely rooftop: reduce children, keep distant city soft, bring breath or clothes movement closer. 
Feeling: above everyone, alone for a moment.
Restless city: allow multiple distant elements to keep appearing from different directions while the 
character attempts to sit quietly. Feeling: the city never fully stops.
The location was not “bad for a film.” The missing ingredient was a story decision.<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 24 / 37>LIFE OS | SKILL 01: SOUND | REFERENCE MANUAL

Future Footage Analysis Framework
When raw travel footage exists, this manual should guide analysis before editing. A future assistant 
or collaborator should not jump directly to “cinematic music + whooshes.” It should first reconstruct 
the intended audience experience.

7. What actually happened in the footage?
8. What should the audience feel: peace, tension, effort, nostalgia, scale, loneliness, energy, curiosity,

9. What is the most important change or moment?

10. Which ambience/world sounds establish the place?
11. Which physical actions deserve audible detail?

14. What should be foreground at each moment?
15. What can remain present but quiet?

Adaptive Teaching + Visual Escalation Rule
A technically correct explanation is not automatically a learnable explanation. Sound training must 
adapt to Akshay’s actual understanding in the moment rather than assuming a fixed intelligence 
level or a fixed preferred teaching format.
Treat the following as signals that the current teaching representation is not working well enough:
 Akshay asks substantially the same concept again in different words.
 He can repeat terminology but cannot apply it to a sound decision or real scene.
 He explicitly says the topic feels confusing, heavy, boring, or difficult to read.
 The concept depends strongly on hearing, timing, waveform behavior, spatial perspective, 
before/after comparison, or another relationship that is easier to perceive than to describe.
 The explanation depends on prerequisite ideas that are not yet intuitive.
When these signals appear, do not simply repeat the same explanation with more terminology. 
Change the representation. Use this escalation sequence as appropriate:
1. Restate the idea in one plain sentence.
2. Use one concrete everyday or film-scene example.
3. Compare the correct interpretation with one likely wrong interpretation.
4. Use an audible/visual example, waveform, screen recording, scene breakdown, or practical 
demonstration when seeing or hearing the relationship will make it clearer.
5. When useful, proactively search for a beginner-friendly YouTube/video explanation instead of 
waiting for Akshay to request one.
6. After the video or visual explanation, reconnect it to the exact Sound concept being learned and 
verify understanding with a very small recall question or practical test.
Video-selection difficulty gate
A video is not suitable merely because it is accurate, popular, or made by an expert. Before 
recommending it, judge whether it matches Akshay’s current level. Prefer intuition-first 
explanations, strong visual/audible demonstrations, one concept at a time, minimal unexplained 
jargon, and examples close to the current Sound problem. Reject or defer videos that begin too far 
above the current level, move too fast, depend on technical prerequisites that have not been learned, 
or add complexity that is unnecessary for the present lesson.
Do not force video into every lesson. Conversation, recall, listening exercises, and practical

Appendix 1 - Misconceptions That Were Corrected During 
Learning
This section matters because the learning journey was not a straight list of definitions. Several ideas 
became clear only after a wrong or incomplete rule was challenged by a new example. If the same 
confusion returns later, use this appendix to repair it quickly.
Old rule / confusion Correction
Foreground = loud / single sound
False. Foreground is intended audience 
attention. A soft breath can be foreground; a 
loud engine can remain background.
Ambience = many mixed continuous sounds
False. One fan hum can help establish a room. 
Ambience is about place/world, not number or 
continuity.
Character attention defines foreground
Incomplete. The audience can be directed 
toward a sound the character never notices, 
such as a bomb ticking behind a sleeping 
person.
If a sound becomes clearer, its perspective 
becomes close
False. A far waterfall can become clearer and 
foreground while remaining far.
Louder = closer False. A whisper can be close and quiet; thunder
can be far and loud.
Silence = any reduction in sound
False. Quieter ambience is not silence. One layer
disappearing while others continue is selective 
absence/contrast.
Quiet = peaceful
False. Quiet can create peace, dread, loneliness, 
suspense, awe, or awkwardness depending on 
context.
Break = horror
False. A rhythm break means change. The story 
decides whether that change means arrival, 
relief, comedy, tension, or something else.
Unrealistic sound = bad sound
False. Unrealistic sound can be strongly 
motivated by story, emotion, comedy, 
surrealism, or style.
Sound design starts after video
False. Foley often needs picture, but sound is 
planned before, captured during, and 
constructed/refined after.
All real sounds should be included for 
immersion
False. Reality supplies more information than a 
story needs. Immersion depends on hierarchy 
and intentional selection, not equal 
reproduction of everything.
Changing emotion requires changing the event
False. Keep the same visuals and plot first; try 
changing sound hierarchy, rhythm, perspective, 
contrast, and internal treatment.<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 33 / 37>LIFE OS | SKILL 01: SOUND | REFERENCE MANUAL

Appendix 2 - Scenario Bank for Recall and Practice
These scenarios are designed for future recall sessions. Do not read the answers from the manual 
first. Pick one scenario, close the document, and reason through it in your own words.
Railway platform at 5 AM
World: announcements, birds, trolley wheels, train bed. A far horn arrives early before train picture. 
Decide whether it stays ambience or becomes far + foreground.
Busy market to flute player
Start with rich market ambience. Gradually let one flute become focus without necessarily making it 
physically close. Decide whether the flute lingers as you walk away.
Rain + phone call
Rain establishes world. Phone can become focus. After shocking news, compare objective rain with 
internal/subjective treatment.
Hotel at 4 AM
AC + faint traffic as world; bag/shoes as action; knock as focus. Design both shock version and dread 
version without changing visuals.
Night train
Use ka-dunk repetition for comfort; break one expected beat for tension. Then redesign the same 
rhythm for a peaceful travel montage.
Mountain summit
Footsteps + breathing create progress rhythm. Final step and removal of that rhythm communicate 
arrival while wind continues.
Festival to side street
Use busy -> sparse contrast. Design the exact same side street once as peaceful and once as 
dangerous.
Mountain guesthouse window
Indoor filtered world changes when window opens. Far waterfall can become foreground. Then build
packing, stairs, door, wind, motorcycle perspective.
Late-night office message
Separate real-world sounds, story-motivated phone vibration, and emotional heartbeat/muffling.

Appendix 3 - Professional Video Observation Worksheet
When watching a behind-the-scenes sound video or an actual film scene, do not force every lesson to 
appear. Use this worksheet to record what you genuinely notice.
 What did I notice before I tried naming any lesson?
 What sound seems to establish the world?
 What sound is the intended foreground, and what makes me believe that?
 What feels close, medium, or far? Does the visual distance match the sound distance?
 Is there a repeated pattern? What expectation does it create?
 What changes or disappears? What contrast does that create?
 Why does each emphasized sound deserve to exist?
 Does sound continue over a cut, arrive before the next picture, or match another sound?
 Is any sound clearly memory or internal/subjective rather than present physical reality?
 What raw object/material was recorded? What sonic quality did it contribute?
 What did the professional do that I would not have thought of myself?
